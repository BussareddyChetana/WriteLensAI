How It Works

From pasted text to explained verdict.

A walk through exactly what happens between hitting "Analyze" and seeing your result — nothing is hidden behind a black box.

1

Input your content

Paste plain text directly, or upload a .txt, .pdf, or .docx file. Text is extracted automatically and normalized (Unicode cleanup, whitespace collapsing) before anything else happens.

2

Feature extraction

Two families of features are computed in parallel: TF-IDF word and bigram frequencies (what's being said), and stylometrics — sentence-length standard deviation, type-token ratio, average word length, punctuation density, and transition-word density (how it's being said). Combining both is what keeps the model from just memorizing topic vocabulary instead of writing style.

3

ML inference

The combined feature vector is scaled and passed to the trained classifier (selected automatically at training time from Logistic Regression, Linear SVM, and Random Forest candidates based on held-out F1 score). The model outputs a raw probability of "AI-generated."

4

Confidence calibration

Very short inputs get pulled toward the uninformative 50/50 midpoint, since a few words carry almost no reliable stylistic signal. The final probability is also capped — WriteLens AI will never report 99%+ certainty in either direction, because no statistical model deserves that much trust on open-ended, real-world text. If the result sits within 15 points of a coin-flip, we report "Uncertain" rather than force a side.

5

Explanation & source estimate

Alongside the verdict, the same stylometric measurements are used to generate plain-language reasons ("uniform sentence lengths," "high vocabulary diversity") and an experimental estimate of which AI model family the writing pattern most resembles.

6

Review & export

Everything — the verdict, per-sentence breakdown, writing statistics, and source estimate — is saved to your dashboard and available as a downloadable PDF audit report at any time.

Why the honesty matters

We'd rather be right than sound certain

Text classifiers trained even on large datasets can be overconfident on writing that doesn't closely resemble their training examples. Capping displayed confidence and introducing an explicit "Uncertain" verdict are deliberate design choices — not limitations we're hiding.
